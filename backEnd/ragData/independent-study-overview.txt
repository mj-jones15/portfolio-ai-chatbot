My independent study project, advised by Dr. Brent Harrison at the University of Kentucky, focused on analyzing how Responsible AI (RAI) research influences national and international AI governance frameworks. The project combined natural language processing, embedding-based semantic analysis, and large-scale citation mapping to evaluate the relationship between academic RAI literature and real-world policy documents.

To build the dataset, I collected papers from major AI ethics conferences including NeurIPS and AIES, alongside national and international AI governance frameworks from organizations such as UNESCO, the United States, China, and Egypt. Using the Semantic Scholar API, I constructed an end-to-end data pipeline that extracted metadata, abstracts, citation relationships, and keyword distributions across a 20,000+ paper corpus. I then generated semantic embeddings using the intfloat/e5-large-v2 model and evaluated policy documents against academic literature using cosine similarity scoring and citation overlap analysis.

One of the project’s major findings was that Egypt and UNESCO demonstrated the strongest research grounding, with Egypt leading the dataset at 48 highly aligned papers connected to its governance framework. In contrast, China showed the highest density of Responsible AI terminology in policy language, but had zero papers surpassing the research consensus threshold, representing the largest gap between topical ambition and measurable academic grounding. U.S. policy documents clustered in the middle of the dataset, showing moderate alignment with the broader research landscape while still leaving room for stronger academic integration.

Crucially, the project revealed a three-way misalignment between research and policy: fairness overwhelmingly dominated academic RAI paper production, explainability received the strongest citation influence across the literature, while accountability emerged as the dominant concept in policy language itself. Sustainability, despite appearing in many governance frameworks, was nearly absent from both research production and citation impact, suggesting a significant blind spot in the current Responsible AI ecosystem.

I presented these findings at the 20th Annual University of Kentucky Showcase of Undergraduate Scholars under the paper title "The Translation Between Responsible AI Research and AI Legislation".
